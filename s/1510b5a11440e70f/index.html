Better Pelicans

We ask AI models to draw a pelican riding a bicycle 🚲, then we judge the results with entirely unreasonable rigor.

Experiment 0001 is complete — it produced a provisional canonical pelican, currently sitting in the gallery awaiting the owner's blessing. We make no claims about model training influence, because we can't know and won't pretend otherwise.

The test

Since October 2024, one modest sentence has served as our informal exam for text-to-SVG models:

Generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle.

Full credit — and full blame — goes to Simon Willison. In October 2024 he asked a model to draw a pelican on a bicycle, discovered it was much harder than it sounds, and wrote it up on his blog with a matching benchmark repository. That post is the entire reason this project exists — no Simon, no pelican benchmark, no us. To be clear: Simon didn't create this website, doesn't run it, and isn't affiliated with it in any way. We simply borrowed his excellent idea and took it far too seriously.

It's an easy sentence and a brutally hard drawing: two distinct objects, a mechanically plausible interaction between them, correct depth ordering, and a valid, standalone, editable vector file. In plain terms, we're asking for a small miracle in XML. See docs/benchmark.md for the canonical wording, variants and sources.

Why this test

Getting from that prompt to a genuinely good SVG — a recognizable pelican, a coherent bicycle, and an unambiguous act of riding — is a real competence test for the models: composition, physics, depth and craftsmanship all at once, in a file you could open and edit.

The benchmark has been public since 2024, so models may well have seen it — or its results — during training. Our pelican may have peeked at the answers. That makes this prompt a useful lens on benchmark contamination too: if a model that likely saw the prompt draws a flawless pelican while a fresh one flails, that's worth knowing. We measure what we can and disclose what we cannot.

What we hope to see

The hoped-for results are specific:

  • A pelican that reads as a pelican — pouch, beak, dignity — on a bicycle that could actually roll.
  • Bird and bike sharing a scene with honest depth: bird on the saddle, feet on the pedals.
  • Newer models beating older ones, a sign the field is actually improving.
  • Repair rounds turning near-misses into keepers — and an honest record of the ones we can't save.
  • A visible contamination signal, if one exists. If the models that likely saw the prompt do suspiciously well, we'll say so out loud.

How we test

Fixed prompt, documented settings, 8–12 independent candidates, deterministic validation, specialist critique, up to three repair rounds, and only then the publication checklist. Every artifact is hashed and logged; future archaeologists will love us. The full procedure is on the How we test page.
